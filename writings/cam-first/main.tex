\begin{abstract}
A released sign-to-text checkpoint bundles a visual encoder, a language-model
state and the optimization history that produced them, so comparing two intact
checkpoints cannot say which part is responsible for a transfer result. We take
the bundle apart in low-resource Taiwan Sign Language by rebuilding models from
parts of Uni-Sign checkpoints trained on CSL-to-Chinese and ASL-to-English data.
The measured benefit of a fixed released visual encoder depends on which mT5
parameters accompany it: $\VisualOnCslMtfiveLexDeltaAbs$ points of content-word
accuracy alongside the mT5 parameters released with it, but
$\VisualOnBaseMtfiveLexDelta$ points alongside untouched mT5-base. Adding the
shared pose-pretraining stage as a third level of the visual factor shows that
benefit to be generic rather than CSL-specific. Interventions localize much of
this sensitivity to one tensor, the output projection, which here is untied from
the input embedding and holds a third of the distinct mT5 parameters. Installing
mT5-base's projection into How2Sign raises accuracy by
$\HeadBaseGainLexDeltaAbs$ points and restores dependence on clip identity,
replicating over three folds on two further recipients, while the reverse swap
removes both from CSL-Daily. Neither a random projection, a rescaled one, nor the
same donor grafted after fine-tuning reproduces the effect---and permuting the
donor's vocabulary rows, which preserves every global property of the matrix and
destroys only which token each row scores, removes most of it. A higher learning
rate partly substitutes for the intervention, so projection initialization is an
optimization-sensitive checkpoint variable rather than a complete account of
transfer. Absolute translation quality stays low throughout; the contribution is
diagnostic, not a competitive TSL system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Work on low-resource sign language translation (SLT) usually starts from a
released checkpoint, and that checkpoint is not a single object: it carries
visual parameters, a language-model state and a training history, and the three
travel together. When the sign source language and the written output language
also covary across available checkpoints---as they do, since a model trained on
Chinese Sign Language video was trained to emit Chinese text---comparing intact
checkpoints cannot identify which component produced a difference. The confound
arises whenever multilingual and cross-language SLT checkpoints are compared
\citep{signet,mlslt,youtubeslfive}, and in systems assembled from separately
pretrained vision and language components \citep{sltunet,gfsltvlp,sign2gpt}.

Taiwan Sign Language (TSL) makes this concrete. Its corpus translations are
Chinese, but its historical development is tied to Japanese Sign Language rather
than to Chinese Sign Language (CSL) \citep{smith2005,fischergong2010,sutai2009,
sasaki2007}. Uni-Sign \citep{unisign} releases same-architecture checkpoints for
CSL-to-Chinese and ASL-to-English, and none for TSL, so adapting either means
choosing an initialization whose sign-language and written-language provenance
cannot be varied independently. We therefore stop treating checkpoint identity as
a language-level variable and treat it as a bundle of initial conditions that can
be taken apart.

\paragraph{What we vary, and what we call it.}
Throughout, a \emph{visual state} is the pose encoder plus the linear bridge
feeding the decoder, and a \emph{language state} is the mT5 parameters: the
encoder--decoder body plus the \emph{output projection}, the matrix mapping a
final hidden state onto vocabulary logits. In mT5 that projection is ordinarily
tied to the input embedding; in every released Uni-Sign checkpoint it is stored
and trained as a separate \texttt{lm\_head} matrix, and it is that matrix we
replace. We name a state by its source checkpoint, so ``the CSL-Daily language
state'' means the mT5 parameters exactly as released in the CSL-Daily
checkpoint, and ``mT5-base'' the published multilingual model with no
sign-language training. Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} shows the parts and the
conditions that recombine them.

Our approach draws on analyses of what transfers between networks
\citep{neyshabur2020transfer}, model stitching and parameter interventions
\citep{bansal2021stitch,taskarith}, output-embedding specialization
\citep{embcoupling}, and cross-lingual lexical reinitialization
\citep{artetxe2020cross,devries2021good,wechsel,focus2023}; few-sample
fine-tuning motivates caution about optimization sensitivity
\citep{zhang2021few}, and signed-language NLP work emphasizes linguistically
meaningful evaluation and transparent limitations \citep{yin2021signed,
mullergloss}. Unlike reinitialization methods such as WECHSEL and FOCUS, our
vocabulary, tokenizer and architecture never change---only which pretrained
matrix occupies the output projection when fine-tuning begins. We first ask
whether visual pretraining has a fixed value across language states and find that
it does not; we then localize that sensitivity to the output projection, and use
a permutation control, random resets, post-hoc grafts, a learned interface
alignment and a learning-rate sweep to narrow what it can be attributed to. The
causal claim concerns output-projection initialization as an adaptation variable,
not Chinese-versus-English linguistic matching.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data and splits.}
We use the Taiwan Sign Language Corpus \citep{tslcorpus}: \SplitParagraphs\
filmed paragraphs, \SplitUtterances\ annotated utterances with Chinese
translations, and \SplitSigners\ signers. Splits are cut at the paragraph, since
adjacent utterances share signer, topic and often whole clauses; each of three
folds trains on \SplitTrainParas\ paragraphs (\SplitTrainUtts\ utterances) and
tests on \SplitTestParas\ (\SplitTestUtts), with per-fold counts in
Appendix~\ref{sec:repro}. Signer identity is deliberately not a splitting
variable, and at most \SplitTestSignersUnseen\ test signer per fold is unseen in
training, so the evaluation measures generalization to held-out \emph{paragraphs}
rather than held-out \emph{signers}, and we scope our claims accordingly.

\paragraph{The main diagnostic.}
Absolute BLEU is near the floor for every model here, so our primary readout is a
forced-choice diagnostic that stays informative at low translation quality:
\LexItemsN\ four-way items over \LexTypeN\ content words, each varying one
content-word slot of a reference sentence to give a gold candidate and three
distractors matched for corpus part-of-speech, near the gold in log frequency,
and free of exact location-and-handshape collisions
(Appendix~\ref{sec:diag}). For a candidate $c$ tokenized as $y_1,\dots,y_{T}$ and
a video $v$, we teacher-force the complete candidate---conditioning each token on
the candidate's own preceding tokens rather than on the model's output---and
score
\begin{equation}
s(c \mid v) \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\!\left(y_t \mid y_{<t},\, v\right),
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
selecting $\arg\max_c s(c\mid v)$. Because scores are length-normalized means,
the choice is not dominated by candidate length. Absolute accuracy is not a pure
measure of visual grounding: the set is rank-balanced so that mean-token scoring
with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct pins the gold at \ChanceLevel\% \citep{qwen25}, but
other text-only scorers given the sentence and no video reach
\LexMajority--\LexLMpriorSum\%. We therefore treat it as auxiliary and rely on
within-model paired contrasts.

\paragraph{Measuring dependence on the clip.}
To ask whether a trained model's answers depend on the video in front of it, we
use \emph{wrong-clip sensitivity}, written $\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}$: the drop in
content-word accuracy when the target clip is replaced by a different,
duration-matched real clip from a held-out paragraph, leaving sentence and
candidates unchanged. A model reading clip-specific information loses accuracy;
one running a video-independent language prior does not. We substitute a real
clip rather than zeroing the input, because an all-zero pose sequence is out of
distribution and can reverse the sign of an effect. One caution applies
throughout: near-zero $\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}$ says only that behavior does not
depend on clip identity, not that visual information is absent internally, and a
decoder collapsed onto a near-constant output registers near-zero
$\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}$ for that reason alone. We therefore read it alongside free
decoding and the bidirectional interventions. A second, \ItemsN-item
pointing-sign diagnostic gives an independent readout.

\paragraph{Models, interventions and training.}
Uni-Sign pairs a 6.9M-parameter pose branch over COCO-WholeBody keypoints
\citep{cocowholebody,rtmpose} with mT5-base \citep{mt5}. We use the released
CSL-Daily \citep{csldaily}, CSL-News \citep{unisign}, How2Sign \citep{how2sign},
OpenASL \citep{openasl} and WLASL \citep{wlasl} checkpoints, all of which inherit
Uni-Sign's stage-1 CSL-News pose pretraining---so ``the CSL-Daily visual state''
means the released state \emph{after} that shared stage, a distinction we test in
\S\ref{sec:factorial}. Of 582.4M distinct mT5 parameters, the untied
$250{,}112\times768$ output projection accounts for \HeadParamsM{}M, or 33\%.
\textsc{rand-vis} keeps mT5-base and leaves the pose branch untrained.

Unless stated otherwise, every condition is fine-tuned end-to-end for 12 epochs
under Uni-Sign's published recipe (pose rate $3\times10^{-4}$, mT5 rate
$3\times10^{-5}$) over three paragraph folds; key projection interventions are
additionally rerun under three seeds on fold 0. Confidence intervals use
paragraph-clustered bootstrap resampling and paired contrasts use
paragraph-level permutation tests, so both respect the clustering the splits
induce.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The value of a visual state depends on its language state}
\label{sec:factorial}

Table~\ref{tab:factorial} crosses three visual states with two language states.
The released CSL-Daily visual state is worth \VisualOnCslMtfiveLexDeltaAbs\
points of content-word accuracy beside the language state released with it
(\VisualOnCslMtfiveLexCI), and $\VisualOnBaseMtfiveLexDelta$ points beside
untouched mT5-base (\VisualOnBaseMtfiveLexCI); the
\InteractionAdaptLexDeltaAbs-point interaction (\InteractionAdaptLexCI,
$p\InteractionAdaptLexCIPclust$) is positive in all three rerun seeds. This is
not evidence that cross-sign-language visual transfer is absent, but that its
\emph{measured} value is conditional on what accompanies it: the same encoder
looks valuable or worthless depending on a choice a checkpoint comparison never
surfaces. Wrong-clip sensitivity behaves the same way, large in the left column
and near zero in the right.

The middle row answers a question a two-row version of this table could not.
Because every released Uni-Sign visual state inherits the same stage-1 CSL-News
pose pretraining, the top row's advantage could come from that shared stage or
from CSL-Daily's adaptation on top of it. Holding the language state fixed at
CSL-Daily's, the stage-1 visual state alone is worth
\StageOneOnCslMtfiveLexDeltaAbs\ points over an untrained branch
(\StageOneOnCslMtfiveLexCI), while adding CSL-Daily's adaptation changes accuracy
by \StageOneAdaptLexDelta\ points (\StageOneAdaptLexCI). Under mT5-base the same
comparison yields \StageOneOnBaseMtfiveLexDelta\ points
(\StageOneOnBaseMtfiveLexCI), so the interaction reproduces with the shared stage
in place of the adapted state (\InteractionStageOneLexDeltaAbs\ points,
\InteractionStageOneLexCI). Essentially all of the conditional visual benefit is
already present in generic pose pretraining and almost none is specific to
CSL-Daily---a sharper claim than the factorial alone supports. Swapping the two
Chinese-output checkpoints' halves against each other likewise leaves both near
their intact scores (\CrossNewsDailyLexLocal\% and \CrossDailyNewsLexLocal\%), so
near-intact performance needs no jointly trained pair. What, then, about these
language states matters?

\subsection{Localizing the sensitivity to the output projection}
\label{sec:head}

Released input embeddings sit only 0.001--0.003 from mT5-base in relative $L_2$,
whereas released output projections sit 0.925--0.980 away; we use that
displacement only to choose what to intervene on first, not as a claim that
distance causes anything. Replacing only How2Sign's output projection with
mT5-base's, changing nothing else, raises content-word accuracy by
\HeadBaseGainLexDeltaAbs\ points
(\HeadBaseGainLexCI) and wrong-clip sensitivity by \HeadClipBaseGainLex\ points
(\HeadClipBaseGainLexCI). The converse is destructive: giving CSL-Daily
How2Sign's projection lowers accuracy from \CsldailyLexLocal\% to
\HeadCslHowLexLocal\% and $\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}$ from \CsldailyLexSwapDelta\ to
\HeadCslHowLexSwapDelta\ points---a residual wrong-clip effect that sits
\HeadClipKillVsRandLex\ points from \textsc{rand-vis}'s
(\HeadClipKillVsRandLexCI), i.e.\ indistinguishable from a model that never saw
sign-language pretraining at all. Both directions replicate across three seeds.

A complement control separates the projection from the rest of the language
state: giving How2Sign all of CSL-Daily's mT5 parameters \emph{except} the output
projection---774.5M, four times as many---reaches only \BodyHowCslLexLocal\%
with $\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}=\BodyHowCslLexSwapDelta$ and stays degenerate under
free decoding. The far larger surrounding component does not reproduce the
rescue, so much of the language-side sensitivity is attributable to the output
projection, without that tensor being the only one that matters.

\subsection{What property of a projection makes it a good initialization?}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Localizing \emph{where} the sensitivity lives does not say \emph{what} about a
successful projection matters, because several properties covary across the
donors used so far: being pretrained at all, overall scale, per-script row
statistics, and whether rows stand in the token correspondence they were trained
in. Scale and reset are already excluded. A norm-matched random projection lands
\HeadRandVsBaseLexDeltaAbs\ points below mT5-base's (\HeadRandVsBaseLexCI), so
removing the English-specialized matrix is not by itself sufficient; two random
initializations whose row norms differ by a factor of 2.2 are indistinguishable;
and rescaling How2Sign's own projection moves accuracy by at most about a point
(Appendix~\ref{sec:donors}).

A row permutation settles the rest. We permute the vocabulary rows of mT5-base's
projection before fine-tuning, which preserves the multiset of rows exactly---and
with it the Frobenius norm, the row-norm distribution, the singular values and
hence the conditioning---while destroying which token each pretrained row scores.
The input embedding is untouched, so the model still reads the vocabulary
correctly and only its output side is scrambled. If generic pretrained structure
were what the recipient needs, this donor should rescue as well as the intact one.

It does not. The permuted projection reaches \HeadHowPermLexLocal\% against
\HeadHowBaseLexLocal\% for the same matrix unpermuted---a shortfall of
\HeadPermVsBaseLexDeltaAbs\ points (\HeadPermVsBaseLexCI)---with wrong-clip
sensitivity of only \HeadHowPermLexSwapDelta\ points. Two further comparisons
place what survives: it sits within \HeadPermVsRandLexCI\ points of a
norm-matched \emph{random} projection and within \HeadPermGainLexCI\ of the
recipient's own English-specialized one. A pretrained matrix whose rows no longer
match their tokens is thus worth about as much as no pretrained matrix at all,
and permuting within script groups, which also preserves per-script norm
structure, does not recover the difference (\HeadHowPermWsLexLocal\%,
\HeadPermWsVsBaseLexCI\ against the intact donor).

What a useful projection supplies is therefore not generic pretrained structure
but rows standing in their trained correspondence with the tokens they
score---though this does not isolate lexical semantics, since that correspondence
and mT5's token-specific geometry remain entangled. Consistently, the donors that
rescue vary widely in provenance---a gloss-to-Chinese projection trained with no
video rescues How2Sign---yet rescue tracks neither video provenance nor distance
from mT5-base.

\subsection{The effect replicates on further recipients}
\label{sec:generality}

Because the result so far rests on one deeply analyzed recipient, we replicated
the protocol in full on two others over the same three folds
(Table~\ref{tab:head}): OpenASL, a second sentence-level ASL-to-English
translation model, and WLASL, pretrained for isolated-sign recognition rather
than translation and so the harder test. Both replicate. Installing mT5-base's
projection is worth \HeadBaseGainOpenLexDeltaAbs\ points on OpenASL
(\HeadBaseGainOpenLexCI) and \HeadBaseGainWlaslLexDeltaAbs\ on WLASL
(\HeadBaseGainWlaslLexCI); CSL-Daily's gives \HeadCslGainOpenLexDeltaAbs\ and
\HeadCslGainWlaslLexDeltaAbs\ points, and wrong-clip sensitivity rises from near
zero alongside accuracy throughout. The phenomenon is thus a property of this
class of checkpoint rather than of How2Sign, though still specific to an
architecture whose output projection is untied and unusually large.

\subsection{The intervention acts through adaptation, not at final readout}
\label{sec:posthocmain}

Timing rules out the simplest remaining explanation, that the donor is merely a
better final readout for a representation the recipient learns anyway.
CSL-Daily's projection gives a \HeadHowCslLexGapAbs-point gain installed
\emph{before} fine-tuning but changes How2Sign by \PostHowCslPostDelta\ points
when grafted \emph{afterwards}, with wrong-clip sensitivity staying near zero
across three seeds (\PostHowCslLexSwapDelta\ points, \PostHowCslSwapCI). A
learned interface sharpens this: a $768\times768$ ridge map fitted on held-out
development states lowers the donor's dev cross-entropy below the recipient's own
projection and recovers 11.8 accuracy points, yet $\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}$ stays
near zero. Repairing a lexical readout without restoring dependence on clip
identity is hard to reconcile with a linear readout-mismatch account
(Appendix~\ref{sec:posthoc}): the donor must be present as the model adapts.

\subsection{Generation recovers, and optimization is a second axis}
\label{sec:optim}

The same ordering appears under unconstrained decoding, where the failure modes
are qualitatively distinct rather than merely low-scoring. How2Sign with its own
projection gives BLEU \HowtwosignBleu\ with \HowtwosignUniq\% distinct
outputs---a decoder emitting essentially one string---against \HeadHowBaseBleu\
and \HeadHowBaseUniq\% with mT5-base's; a random projection lands between them,
recycling stock fragments (Appendix~\ref{sec:working}). CSL-Daily improves from
\CsldailyBleu\ to \HeadCslBaseBleu\ and in reverse collapses to
\HeadCslHowBleu\ with \HeadCslHowModal\% modal output. What the intervention
buys is an escape from generation collapse, not high-quality translation.

We report both because they answer different questions: Equation~\ref{eq:score}
scores each token given the \emph{candidate's} own prefix, so a model can rank
continuations above chance yet be unable to produce a sentence.

Finally, a learning-rate sweep bounds any claim about the intact ASL checkpoint.
At an mT5 rate of $3\times10^{-4}$ its comparison against \textsc{rand-vis} is
\LrThreeEFourVsRandvisLexDelta\ points (\LrThreeEFourVsRandvisLexCI), so the two
become indistinguishable and ``ASL pretraining is worse than none'' is an artifact
of the default rate rather than a stable ranking. The projection effect survives,
still adding \HeadGainAtLrThreeEFourLexDeltaAbs\ points at that rate
(\HeadGainAtLrThreeEFourLexCI); see Appendix~\ref{sec:optapp}.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}

Four results carry the claim. The factorial shows that the measured value of a
fixed visual state changes with the language state accompanying it, and that what
is conditionally valuable is generic pose pretraining rather than adaptation to a
particular sign language. Bidirectional projection interventions rescue three ASL
recipients and damage CSL-Daily, while the four-times-larger complement does not.
The row permutation shows that a useful projection supplies rows in their trained
correspondence with tokens, not generic pretrained structure. And the timing
contrast shows the donor must be present during adaptation.

The boundaries matter as much: we identify no causal language-match effect, no
semantic visual grounding, and no causal order between generation collapse and
wrong-clip sensitivity. Still, a released checkpoint is not indivisible evidence
of cross-language transfer, and decomposing its initialization is what makes such
claims testable.

\section*{Limitations}

The evidence comes from one TSL corpus, one Uni-Sign/mT5 architecture, and one
low-resource setting. Checkpoint provenance and written output language are not
orthogonal across the released checkpoints, so we do not infer a
Chinese-versus-English language effect. The \HeadParamsM{}M-parameter untied
projection is architecture-specific.

Our splits are cut at the paragraph and signer identity is not a splitting
variable, so with at most \SplitTestSignersUnseen\ unseen test signer per fold
the evaluation measures generalization to held-out paragraphs and not to held-out
signers. Whether these effects survive a genuine cross-signer split is untested,
and that is the more demanding condition for any claim about deployment.

The row permutation narrows the mechanism but does not close it. It shows that
row-to-token correspondence is necessary, yet lexical-semantic organization and
other token-specific structure built by mT5 pretraining remain entangled within
that correspondence, so we can say which property is required without saying
which aspect of it does the work.

The forced-choice diagnostic retains substantial language-side signal: matched
text-only scores span \LexMajority--\LexLMpriorSum\%, rising to \LexLMcontext\%
with extra discourse context. The data do not fully separate wrong-clip
sensitivity from generation collapse, since collapse itself reduces measured
input sensitivity, and BLEU remains low after generation is restored. Some
auxiliary donors, learning-rate cells and mechanistic probes use fold~0 only. The
learning-rate result shows that adaptation dynamics, not initialization alone,
shape these outcomes. Label smoothing and other hyperparameters are not
separately ablated.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

The Taiwan Sign Language Corpus \citep{tslcorpus} is a research-use beta release,
and we redistribute no corpus material or signer metadata; the split statistics
we report are aggregate counts only. Pose keypoints can retain identity cues and
are treated as personal data. The location-and-handshape filter is an automated
corpus check, not linguistic validation, and cannot replace review by fluent TSL
signers or Deaf community participation \citep{yin2021signed,mullergloss}. No
Deaf signers participated in our item design or analysis, so we make no usability
or community-benefit claims.

\section{Diagnostics and statistical procedure}
\label{sec:diag}

The content-word diagnostic replaces one reference slot with the gold word or one
of three distractors. Gold and distractor tokens share the corpus POS tag, and
distractor types are selected near the gold in log frequency. Candidates must be
standalone tokens under jieba and must not share the gold sign's location and
handshape signature. That filter removes exact sign-phonological collisions, but
near-minimal pairs remain: an audit finds 16.6\% of distractors share the gold
location, 6.3\% share its handshape, and 47.0\% of items contain at least one
such distractor. These are automated corpus checks, not human judgments.

The gold occupies each Qwen rank equally often, which makes the
construction-specific Qwen mean-log-probability baseline exactly \ChanceLevel\%.
That guarantee does not generalize to other scorers. Under the same
sentence-level input, Qwen with summed log probability reaches \LexLMpriorSum\%,
a GLM-family scorer \citep{glm4} reaches \LexLMtwoPrior\%, and a unigram prior
reaches \LexMajority\%. With preceding sentences from the same paragraph Qwen
reaches \LexLMcontext\%, but the video conditions receive no such discourse
context, so this is an unmatched reference.

\textsc{swap} replaces the target with a real utterance from another held-out
paragraph, matched in duration; the median duration difference is
\LexSwapDurMedian\,ms and results average over three donor clips. A
within-paragraph donor clip, which holds signer and session fixed, still costs
CSL-Daily \CsldailyLexWithinDeltaAbs\ points (\CsldailyWithinCI), so the effect is
not explained by session or broad topic. Zeroing the clip is not used as the
primary measure because it is out of distribution and can reverse the sign of an
effect.

\paragraph{The pointing-sign diagnostic.}
The second diagnostic contains \ItemsN\ four-way items targeting pointing signs,
built by the same procedure with the varied slot at a referential expression
rather than a content word. It is more predictable from text alone---a model with
no video reaches a higher baseline than on the content-word set---so absolute
accuracies are less informative here, but the wrong-clip effects it resolves are
substantially \emph{larger}, which is why we report both sets.

Every intervention in the main text points the same way on this set. Intact
How2Sign scores \HowtwosignLocal\% with
$\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}=\HowtwosignPronSwapDelta$; installing mT5-base's projection
raises it to \HeadHowBaseLocal\% with
$\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}=\HeadHowBasePronSwapDelta$, against
\HeadHowBaseLexSwapDelta\ on the content-word set. In the reverse direction
intact CSL-Daily scores \CsldailyLocal\% with
$\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}=\CsldailyPronSwapDelta$, falling to \HeadCslHowLocal\% and
$\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}=\HeadCslHowPronSwapDelta$ under How2Sign's projection---the
clip dependence is abolished outright rather than merely reduced. Frame
shuffling, which removes temporal order while leaving the frame distribution
intact, costs intact CSL-Daily \CsldailyPronShuffleDeltaAbs\ points and the
reverse-swapped cell \HeadCslHowPronShuffleDeltaAbs.

The two item sets therefore agree on the direction and the localization of every
intervention we report, differing mainly in how much of the available wrong-clip
effect each resolves. \S\ref{sec:optapp} notes the one place they disagree, which
concerns additivity with the learning rate rather than the projection effect
itself.

All confidence intervals in the main content-word results use 10{,}000 bootstrap
samples resampled over paragraphs, and paired contrasts use paragraph-level
sign-flip permutation tests. The resampling unit is therefore the paragraph, not
the item: a pooled three-fold content-word contrast spans \LexItemsN\ items
drawn from \LexClustersN\ paragraphs, and it is that cluster count, not the item
count, that sets the effective sample size of every interval we quote. Holm
correction is applied to the original family of interval-quoted contrasts; the
component interaction remains significant after correction, while the small
encoder-only content-word contrast does not.

\section{Post-hoc readout tests}
\label{sec:posthoc}

A post-hoc graft re-scores a fully trained recipient without further training.
Across three How2Sign seeds, grafting CSL-Daily's projection changes accuracy by
$-0.6$, $+1.5$ and $-3.6$ points, with score-margin changes of $-0.223$, $-0.209$
and $-0.207$ nats. The self-graft control changes little, and a post-hoc
mT5-base projection also gives small mixed effects across three seeds. The same
mT5-base projection at initialization improves How2Sign by
\HeadBaseGainLexDeltaAbs\ points and CSL-Daily by about 6.5.

We additionally fit $768\times768$ interface maps on held-out development states,
to test whether a donor projection simply expects a different hidden-state basis.
How2Sign and CSL-Daily decoder states are indeed far apart. A ridge map lowers
the CSL-Daily donor's dev cross-entropy on How2Sign states below the recipient's
own projection and recovers about 12 points of contrastive accuracy, yet
$\Delta_{\mathrm{sw}}$ stays near zero; related orthogonal and
cross-entropy-trained maps show the same dissociation. On CSL-Daily the same kind
of aligned graft leaves a reduced but nonzero wrong-clip effect. The alignment
can therefore repair a lexical readout while leaving measured wrong-clip
sensitivity near zero in the trained How2Sign endpoint.

\section{Optimization and model-selection controls}
\label{sec:optapp}

The mT5 learning-rate sweep changes the interpretation of the intact ASL baseline
but not the projection effect. On fold 0, How2Sign with its own projection scores
\HowtwosignLrOneEFourLexLocal\% at $10^{-4}$ and
\HowtwosignLrThreeEFourLexLocal\% at $3\times10^{-4}$; with mT5-base's projection
the corresponding cells reach \HeadHowBaseLrOneEFourLexLocal\% and
\HeadHowBaseLrThreeEFourLexLocal\%. The paired projection effects are large at
all three rates, and at $3\times10^{-4}$ the projection is still worth
\HeadGainAtLrThreeEFourLexDeltaAbs\ points.

The two item sets agree that a higher rate can restore wrong-clip sensitivity but
not about additivity. On content words the projection and learning-rate effects
remain separable. On pointing signs the higher rate alone recovers most of the
available wrong-clip effect, and the additional neutral-projection gain does not
reach $p<0.05$. We therefore avoid claiming a universal additive mechanism.

Model selection is not responsible for the projection effect. Three cells were
rerun while saving the final epoch in addition to the best-dev checkpoint. The
effect is \SelHeadAtBestLexDeltaAbs\ points at the best-dev epochs and
\SelHeadAtLastLexDeltaAbs\ points at epoch 12 (\SelHeadAtLastLexCI). The
How2Sign own-projection run in fact selects an earlier epoch than the rescued
run, so reading both at the same final epoch does not remove the gap.

\section{Gradient probe}
\label{sec:grad}

Before any parameter update, we backpropagate one training step through
\GradProbeBatches\ shared batches for eight recipient/projection combinations,
measuring the visual-side share
$\lVert(g_{\mathrm{pose}},g_{\mathrm{bridge}})\rVert/\lVert g_{\mathrm{total}}\rVert$,
where $g_{\mathrm{bridge}}$ is the pose-to-mT5 bridge gradient. The four
configurations that later collapse lie between 0.390 and \GradDeadMaxShare; the
four that later avoid collapse lie between \GradWorksMinShare\ and 0.634. The
ordering has no overlap, but it should not be read as a continuous predictor:
CSL-Daily with mT5-base's projection has a lower visual share than intact
CSL-Daily while reaching higher final accuracy. The probe shows only that the
training signal begins differently.

Absolute gradients clarify two distinct failures. How2Sign with a random
projection has a very large pose gradient (\GradHowRandPose) but an even larger
mT5-body gradient (\GradHowRandBody), so its visual share is low because the
decoder requires repair. CSL-Daily with How2Sign's projection instead reduces the
pose gradient to \GradCslHowPose\ from \GradCslOwnPose\ under its own projection,
a small absolute gradient on the visual side. Across-batch gradient cosine stays
near zero in all conditions, so gradient coherence does not distinguish success
from collapse.

degenerate, and the output projection remains strongly anchored to where it
started.

\section{Checkpoint provenance and reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

\paragraph{Splits.}
The corpus holds \SplitParagraphs\ paragraphs (\SplitMonoParas\ monologue,
\SplitDialogueParas\ dialogue), \SplitUtterances\ utterances and \SplitSigners\
signers. Across the three folds, training takes \SplitTrainParas\ paragraphs
(\SplitTrainUtts\ utterances), development \SplitDevParas\ (\SplitDevUtts), and
test \SplitTestParas\ (\SplitTestUtts). Folds are balanced on paragraph type and
diagnostic-item count by greedy largest-first assignment, with ties broken on a
hash of the paragraph identifier, so they are deterministic. Signer identity is
not used as a splitting variable: at most \SplitTestSignersUnseen\ test signer
per fold does not also appear in that fold's training split, so these are
held-out-paragraph splits and not cross-signer ones. We report signer allocation
only in aggregate, consistent with redistributing no signer metadata.

\paragraph{Provenance and hyperparameters.}
Uni-Sign initializes each downstream sign checkpoint from stage-1 pose-only
pretraining on CSL-News. The released mT5 bodies stay close to this common
ancestor while \texttt{lm\_head} moves much further: input embeddings shift only
about 0.001--0.003 in relative $L_2$ from mT5-base, released output projections
about 0.925--0.980. Script-wise row norms also differ between CSL and ASL
checkpoints, but the random, rescaling and permutation controls in
Appendix~\ref{sec:donors} are inconsistent with script scale as a sufficient
explanation of the training-time rescue.

Unless otherwise noted, training uses AdamW, batch size 8 with two-step gradient
accumulation, a pose-branch learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$, an mT5 learning
rate of $3\times10^{-5}$, 5\% warmup and label smoothing 0.2. Clips are capped at
192 frames and targets at 64 tokens. The learning-rate sweep changes only the mT5
rate. Cells are selected by development cross-entropy, with the final-epoch
control of Appendix~\ref{sec:optapp}. All reported distractor candidates are
unseen as diagnostic labels during training.
Code, analysis scripts and the per-run measurements behind every number reported
here are available at \url{https://github.com/lopentu/wslp-transfer-artifact}.
The diagnostic items are released as metadata and construction scripts rather
than as text: no corpus material or signer metadata is redistributed, and no
checkpoint weights are, since the donors carry no redistribution licence.
